Specific Heat Capacity of Ice

Heat energy increases the average kinetic energy of the water molecules. They vibrate faster in their fixed crystal lattice, causing the temperature (θ) to rise toward 0°C.

● Specific Heat Capacity (c)

Q = m · c · Δθ

The heat energy required to raise the temperature (θ) of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K) without changing its state. Energy goes into kinetic energy (faster particle motion), so θ rises.

● Latent Heat of Fusion (Lf)

Q = m · Lf  (334 kJ/kg)

The hidden heat required to change 1 kg of solid into liquid at its melting point (0°C) with no change in θ. Energy breaks rigid intermolecular bonds (increasing potential energy).

● Latent Heat of Vaporization (Lv)

Q = m · Lv  (2260 kJ/kg)

The heat required to change 1 kg of liquid into gas at its boiling point (100°C) with no change in θ. Much larger than Lf because molecules must completely separate against attractive forces.

How to Identify Smaller Specific Heat Capacity (c) from the Graph

Gradient = Δθ / ΔQ = 1 / (m · c)

Since mass (m) stays constant at 1 kg throughout heating, the gradient (steepness) of each sloped section is inversely proportional to specific heat capacity (c).

Key Rule: A steeper upward slope means temperature (θ) rises rapidly for a small amount of heat (Q) added — indicating a smaller specific heat capacity (c).

  • Steepest Slope → Gas / Steam (c = 2.0 kJ/kg·°C):
    Has the sharpest upward incline, showing the smallest specific heat capacity among the three states.
  • Second Steepest Slope → Solid / Ice (c = 2.1 kJ/kg·°C):
    Nearly as steep as steam, showing ice also has a small specific heat capacity and warms up quickly per kJ of heat.
  • Gentlest (Least Steep) Slope → Liquid / Water (c = 4.18 kJ/kg·°C):
    Has the shallowest incline, requiring much more heat energy (Q) for each 1°C rise in θ — showing the largest specific heat capacity.
